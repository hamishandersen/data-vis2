Sporting participation in Australia: who, what, when, where and why

Sport participation in Australia

Most Australian adults participate in physical activity in some way, whether by joining a local running club, hitting the gym, or going for a walk. But who participates, which activities are most popular, and how do factors such as age, gender and location influence participation? This webpage explores these questions by comparing two datasets a decade apart: AusPlay 2025 and ABS 2013–14. By examining participation patterns across different demographics and over time, it reveals how Australia's sporting landscape has changed, which trends have persisted, and who may still be missing out.

Section 1

The National Picture

Stacked Bar Chart by Age

How often each age group takes part in sport or physical activity

Source: AusPlay 2025

Older adults lead in daily activity, but over-65s face the highest inactivity gap

Normalising activity frequencies into 100% stacked bars allows us to compare activity habits across age groups on an equal scale, regardless of sample size differences. This layout highlights how regular movement habits shift as Australians transition through different life stages.

People aged 55–64 emerge as the most consistent exercisers, with 57% participating three or more times a week compared to just 48% of 35–44s. However, non-participation surges sharply in later life, with nearly a quarter of adults aged 65+ (24.6%) reporting no activity at all.

The masses love their walking, running and gym

Gym/fitness, recreational walking, and running/jogging consistently rank among the top 3–5 most popular activities across age groups, highlighting their broad appeal across the Australian population. National difference in gender participation

The darker the blue, the more women are participating in sports than men, in that state.

Source: AusPlay 2025

The gender participation gap is almost zero nationally

Using a diverging geographic color scale allows us to instantly visualize the net difference between female and male participation rates across state and territory borders, highlighting regional imbalances that raw totals might obscure.

The gender participation gap is remarkably close to zero across most of the country, heavy population centers like NSW, QLD, SA, and VIC remain tightly aligned within a 1-point margin. However, women strikingly lead by a whopping 10.8 percentage points in the Northern Territory, 5.7 points in the Australian Capital Territory and 4.7 points in Tasmania.

Child Participation across Australia: 2024 vs 2025

An overview of shifts in regular weekly activity across different states and territories.

Source: AusPlay 2024/2025

Kids are finally getting off the couch!

A comparison between 2024 and 2025 reveals a strong nationwide upward trend in youth sports participation, driven by significant surges in several key jurisdictions. South Australia led the growth with a remarkable 7.4% absolute increase, closely followed by the Northern Territory, which surged by 6.1%.

States such as Victoria, New South Wales, Queensland and the Australian Capital Territory showed a marginal increase, Tasmania stood as the sole exception to the trend, experiencing a marginal contraction of 0.3%. Overall, the shifting color gradients across the continent highlight a highly successful period of growing engagement in weekly physical activities for Australian children.

Weekly sports participation across Australia

A comparison of weekly participation rates and estimated numbers of participants across Australian states and territories in 2025.

Source: AusPlay 2025

Participation rates and participant numbers tell different stories

Larger states can have more weekly participants because of their larger populations, while participation rates show the proportion of people who take part. Comparing both measures helps distinguish where sport is most popular proportionally from where the greatest number of people participate.

Despite its smaller population size, Australian Capital Territory has the highest noramlised participation rate across australia with 78.3% of adults participating weekly in sports, with Victoria trailing behind at 70.5%

Section 2

A Decade Apart

State Ranking Shifts Over a Decade

A comparison of state and territory sports participation ranks between ABS (2013-14) and AusPlay (2025).

Source: AusPlay 2025, ABS 2013-2014

Tracking relative shifts rather than raw numbers

Because AusPlay (2025) and the ABS (2013-14) define and measure participation differently, comparing raw percentages directly across decades can be misleading. Looking at changes in state and territory rankings as workaround instead reveals how regional performance has evolved over the decade from 2013/2014 to 2025.

While the Australian Capital Territory holds steady at the top, states like Victoria and Northern Territory have climbed the ranks, reflecting shifting engagement levels.

Age Participation Gap From the National Average

Comparing how far each age group's participation rate sat above or below the national average in 2013-14 versus 2025.

Source: AusPlay 2025, ABS 2013-2014

Centering the national average to spot generational trends

Plotting age groups as a variance above or below the national baseline removes overall population growth distortions in order to highlight generational changes. This approach isolates whether specific age cohorts are becoming more or less active relative to the rest of the country over the decade.

A decade ago, teenagers and young adults sat dramatically higher than the national average, but the 2025 curve has flattened significantly, showing a more evenly distributed participation pattern across age groups

Popularity Shifts of Physical Activities

Tracking the rise and fall of activity rankings between ABS (2013-14) and AusPlay (2025).

Source: AusPlay 2025, ABS 2013-2014

Connecting past and present to reveal changing preferences

Slope graphs use direct visual connecting lines to show how individual categories swap positions between two distinct points in time. This makes it instantly clear which activities have surged in public favor and which have slipped down the ranks over the past ten years.

Traditional team sports such as soccer and netball have experienced mixed movements, while individual flexible fitness pursuits like bush walking and pilates have climbed five or more places to surge in popularity.

Back-to-back horizontal bars mirror women's and men's participation data from a shared central axis, enabling a direct visual comparison of activity preferences. This layout instantly exposes where participation styles overlap and where they diverge.

Section 4: Barriers and Motivations: The Why

Barriers to Physical Activity by Age

The primary reasons non-participants give for not engaging in sport or physical activity

Source: AusPlay 2025

Cross-referencing excuses with life stages

Examining non-participants across multiple age brackets reveals that the obstacles keeping people on the couch change dramatically over a lifetime. Rather than viewing barriers as a monolith, this breakdown links specific hurdles to specific age groups.

Poor health, injury, and aging are the dominant barriers for older Australians, whereas younger non-participants are somewhat more likely to point toward a lack of motivation or perceived laziness. The older one gets, the more the excuse seems to shift from a reason that is intangible to a reason that is tangible

Motivation for Participation for each age group

The primary reasons participants give for engaging in sport or physical activity

Source: AusPlay 2025

Regardless of age, we all particpate for mostly the same reasons

Examining the motivations of participants across age groups reveals that the reasons for engaging in physical activity comes down to a few core categories. One being more physical such as for health and fitness, and to lose weight, and one to have fun and socialise with friends. While the relative importance of these factors may shift slightly with age, the overarching themes remain consistent.

Majority of participants within the 65+ age band expectedly participate to keep fit, whilst motivations like weight loss, training purposes, and performance or competition drop to their lowest levels compared to all younger age brackets.

Participation Variance by Life Factors

How far each demographic group's participation sits above or below the national average (86%)

Source: AusPlay 2025

Those who are weathly and educated appear to be sporty also

Setting the 86% national average as a vertical zero line allows us to immediately identify which life factors drag participation down and which ones elevate it. This diverging structure highlights systemic disparities linked to income, education, and health.

Adults in higher household income brackets ($200k+) and those holding university degrees participate well above the national average, whereas lower income tiers and individuals with chronic health conditions face the largest negative gaps. Not finishing school seems to have the largest negative impact on participation, with a 20.4% gap below the national average.
